A simple, step-by-step manual for everyone. Find your profile below and follow the cards. The French edition is one click away, in the bar above.

Start here

IDevelop helps your organisation track skills, run fair assessments, calibrate talent and drive development. What you can do depends on your profile.

How to read this guide

Where: Read once, before anything else

Two conventions run through the whole manual. 1. The people it names are invented. Norah HARTLEY, Aïcha FARRELL, Zoumana WALSH, Souleymane LARSÈN and the rest do not exist, and their staff numbers start with DEMO- so that nothing is ambiguous. The figures, on the other hand, are the ones the screens actually displayed: the people were replaced, not the measurements. A manual must never let a colleague's talent placement be read — a 9-box cell is learnt through a deliberate disclosure, made by the manager, with a written reason. 2. An absence of measurement is not a zero. Wherever the product writes “—” or “Not measured” it means “never assessed”: it is not a level 0, it is not a gap, and it is never rounded. A 0 is a finding; a “—” is an open question. They call for different actions: “go and measure” and “go and train”.

💡 If you recognise a name in this guide it is a coincidence — and a defect: report it, the rule is that no real person appears here.

New here? Create an account (self-onboarding)

Where: The “Create an account” link on /login (only when self-service onboarding is switched on)

Self-service onboarding is a switch an administrator turns on; it ships off on a fresh installation. When it is on, you can register yourself — but you do not get access immediately: your request waits in an Onboarding queue until an administrator places you into a site, department, service and role (those four are the mandatory fields), which is what actually creates your account. The same applies if you sign in through SSO without an existing account: a request is queued rather than an account created. If your email domain is not on the allow-list, the request is refused by default.

  1. On the login page, click Create an account (if the link is shown).
  2. Enter your work email, your name and a password of at least 12 characters, then submit.
  3. You land on an “awaiting setup” page. An administrator places you into the organization.
  4. Once placed, sign in with the email and password you registered (or via SSO).
💡 No password reaches an inbox and no access is granted until an admin reviews and places you — that is the security measure, not a delay.

Your account: email, notifications, sessions, password, 2FA

Where: Account menu (top-right): My profile (/account) · Notifications (/account/notifications) · Active Sessions (/account/sessions) · Change Password (/change-password) · Two-Factor Auth

Five personal pages, all reachable by every signed-in user — none of them is admin-only. My profile (new in 3.22.85) is where you set your email address: it is the only address the "forgot password" link can reach, so filling it in is what makes self-service recovery possible at all. Notifications holds the email toggle and quiet hours (nothing is dropped, everything is deferred to the end of the window). Active Sessions lists the devices signed in as you, with "Sign out all other sessions (N)". Change Password enforces at least 12 characters with uppercase, lowercase, number and special character — announced on the field and enforced by the server. Two-Factor Auth adds a 6-digit code from an authenticator app plus 10 single-use backup codes.

  1. Open My profile and fill in your email address — do it before you need it.
  2. Open Notifications to choose whether you get email, and to set quiet hours (fill in both times, or leave both empty).
  3. Open Active Sessions after using a shared machine and sign the other devices out.
  4. Use Change Password for a passphrase of 12+ characters; changing it signs out every other session.
  5. Turn on Two-Factor Auth and store the 10 backup codes somewhere other than that phone.
💡 Your name, employee number, role, site, department, service and supervisor are not editable on My profile — that is deliberate. Report an error there to your manager or HR administrator.

Your dashboard — readiness, measured gaps and unmeasured skills

Where: Home / My workspace (landing page after sign-in)

Your landing page shows four summary cards and your full skill detail. On the demo account: 89% ROLE READINESS — 39/49 skills met, 9 SKILL GAPS · 0 critical · 1 Not measured, 9-BOX POSITION: Not placed (“No approved placement yet”), and ASSESSMENT STATE: Not started. The “My skill gaps” table lists every skill your role requires with REQUIRED, CURRENT and GAP. Read it carefully: a row with CURRENT = 0 and GAP = −1 (e.g. Training & Mentorship) means “assessed, level zero”; a row with CURRENT = — and GAP = Not measured (e.g. Budget & Cost Control) means “never assessed”. The second is not counted in the 9 gaps: the product never presents an absence of measurement as a result. The readiness figure only moves on skills that were actually measured.

  1. Open Home (left menu, “My Space”).
  2. Read the four cards: readiness, gaps, 9-box position, assessment state.
  3. In “My skill gaps”, tell −1 / −2 (a measured shortfall to close) apart from Not measured (a skill to get assessed).
  4. Click update self-assessment → or the Start my self-assessment button to act.
  5. Use the quick-action cards at the bottom: My Self-Assessment, My Status, Supervisor reviews, Development plan, Coaching, My profile.
💡 A “Not measured” neither penalises nor protects you: it says nothing at all. Make it disappear by rating the skill — that is the only way to get an honest figure.

Complete your self-assessment

Where: Self-Assessment (left menu)

You rate your current level on every skill your role requires. The scale actually shown at the top of the page is: 0 None · 1 Basic Awareness · 2 Guided · 3 Autonomous · 4 Expert. Until you choose, the dropdown reads “— Choose —”: an unrated skill is therefore not a 0, it is simply empty, and the row's status stays NOT STARTED. The header counter counts rows you actually filled in: on the demo account it reads “0 of 50 skills rated · 50 left to rate”, with an “About 7 min left to finish” estimate. Skills are grouped by pillar (1. HSE & Operational Risk 0/1 · 2. Functional Technical 0/1 · 3. Digital, Data & Work Tools 0/37 · 4. Compliance & Certification 0/2 · 5. Business Acumen 0/6 · 6. People Management 0/3). Columns: SKILL, REQUIRED LEVEL, YOUR SELF-RATING, STATUS, NOTES (free-text box). Filters: All, Not yet rated (50), Critical (0), Below required (0), plus Show my last validated level, Next unrated and Collapse all. Keyboard: 0–4 to rate, J/K or ↑↓ to move. Two buttons: Save Draft and Submit for Review.

  1. Open Self-Assessment from the left menu.
  2. For each skill, replace “— Choose —” with your level: 0 None · 1 Basic Awareness · 2 Guided · 3 Autonomous · 4 Expert.
  3. Add a note in the NOTES column if a concrete example helps your reviewer.
  4. Click Save Draft at any time; the “left to rate” counter falls as you fill rows in.
  5. When everything is filled in, click Submit for Review.
💡 Leaving “— Choose —” is not a neutral option: the skill stays “Not measured” on your dashboard and nobody knows where you stand. An honest 0 is information; a blank is not.

Track your assessment status

Where: My Status (left menu)

This page places your assessment inside the current campaign and inside the review flow. The Current campaign banner shows the cycle and its closing date — on a sample instance: “2026-Q3 — closes on 31/08/2026 PAST THE DEADLINE” — then Where you stand: Not started yet. Three bars quantify progress, all against the skills required: Skills rated 0 of 49, Sent for review 0 of 49, Approved 0 of 49. The table below (SKILL / SELF LEVEL / STATE / COMMENTS / ACTIONS) lists each submitted skill and its state; it reads “No self-assessments yet.” until something is sent. A Continue my self-assessment button takes you back to the form.

  1. Open My Status from the menu.
  2. Read the current campaign and its closing date — a PAST THE DEADLINE badge flags an expired cycle.
  3. Compare the three bars: rated → sent for review → approved.
  4. If a skill comes back as changes requested, reopen the self-assessment, fix it and submit again.
💡 The denominator here is 49, not 50: a skill whose required level is 0 appears in the list to rate but cannot count toward a requirement.

Your reviews and raising a dispute

Where: My reviews (left menu) — /employee/supervisor-reviews

The page shows where each skill you sent currently stands and, once decided, the level retained and the reason for it. The “Where your assessments stand” banner explains the principle: “Every skill you send moves from hand to hand until a decision is made. Here you can see who is holding it right now”. When nothing is in flight the message is explicit: “Nothing pending: none of your skills is currently with a reviewer.”, followed by the Your supervisor's decisions block — on the demo account: “No supervisor reviews available yet.”. As soon as a reviewed row appears, a Dispute button is shown on contestable decisions, and a dispute table (skill, self-rated level, supervisor level, gap, state, final decided rating) follows the case to resolution.

  1. Open My reviews from the menu.
  2. Compare your self-rating with the level the supervisor retained — that gap is what a dispute is about.
  3. If you disagree, click Dispute and argue with facts (what you delivered at that level).
  4. Track the dispute's state in the table below; the final retained level appears there once decided.

Change your password (12 characters minimum)

Where: Account menu → Change Password · /change-password

Three fields — Current Password, New Password, Confirm New Password — and the rule shown under the field: “Must be at least 12 characters with uppercase, lowercase, number, and special character.” This is not just help text: the check is enforced server-side (the validator refuses anything shorter than 12 characters). 💡 A long, unique passphrase meets the 12-character rule with no memory effort — and is reused nowhere else.

What an employee cannot see or do

Where: Everywhere — enforced by the server, not by the menu

An employee's scope is their own record only, and that boundary is enforced by the server, not merely by hiding menu entries. Observed on the running application with the employee signed in: the directory /employees, someone else's record /employees/1 and its edit form /employees/2/edit all answer 403; /api/v1/employees answers 403; another person's readiness /api/v1/employees/1/readiness answers 403 while their own /api/v1/employees/84/readiness answers 200; another person's review history /api/self-assessment/employee/1/movement answers 404 while their own answers 200; the system log /system-logs, admin management /admins, continuity /v2/continuity and compliance /compliance answer 403; approving a self-assessment, their own as much as someone else's (POST /api/self-assessment/employee/…/approve-all), answers 403 — an employee never validates their own rating. Steering screens (the general dashboard, 9-box, PIP, skill matrix, benchmark, report builder, platform sessions, executive surfaces) redirect to /employee/dashboard. Finally, forcing an id into the URL achieves nothing: /employee/dashboard?employeeId=1 does answer 200, but renders “Welcome, Bakary” — your own data. The parameter is ignored; personal pages are always computed from the signed-in user.

How the processes fit together

Each page of the app plays a part in a larger process. These are the end-to-end flows — who does what, in what order, a concrete example, and the practices that make each one work.

🗓️

The assessment campaign — from draft to official level, two state machines advancing together

Who: Admin with “Manage cycles” (creates, opens, locks, closes) · Employee (self-assesses) · Supervisor or Manager (reviews) · Local admin with “Arbitrate disputes” (HR arbitration) · The scheduler (locks at the deadline)
When: Once or twice a year. A campaign has four states: Draft → Open → Locked → Closed (a draft can also be Cancelled with a reason, and a locked campaign reopened with a new deadline). Only launching enrols the participants.
  1. Create the campaign (Administration → Campaign tracking → “New campaign”): unique code, label, dates (closing cannot precede opening). It is born in Draft — nobody is enrolled yet, and the console says so honestly: “Not launched — no participant enrolled”.
  2. Launch it from its console (super administrator). Launching — and only launching — builds the ROSTER: every active employee whose role carries requirements is enrolled, with the FULL department-designed count of expected skills, frozen at launch. Everyone is announced to. The console then switches from “not launched” to a real count.
  3. Each employee opens Self-Assessment, rates 0–4, adds a written justification in the NOTES column where it helps, and submits. A line never rated stays NULL — “not answered yet” is not the rating “None (0)”.
  4. On submission the platform creates the review row and routes it: supervisor if one exists, else manager, else the queue of the admin whose scope covers the person. The review is born empty: no supervisor rating, no gap, no date. Nothing shows as “agreed” before someone has looked.
  5. The reviewer opens the review (state becomes “under review”), then chooses: request changes (comment mandatory, the line returns to the employee), reject (reason mandatory, terminal — and the rating stays UNMEASURED, a rejection is not an agreement), or approve. On approval they may enter their own rating; if it differs from the employee’s, a short reason is required and the save is refused without it.
  6. The approved rating becomes the OFFICIAL LEVEL: written into the skill profile with provenance “supervisor validated”, and the history keeps the before/after. A completed LMS course can raise a skill, but never over a supervisor decision — it stops and records why.
  7. Lock then close. Locking ends data entry and lets reviews finish; it happens automatically at the deadline (“Scheduled jobs” setting), and managers still holding submissions are reminded weekly. A campaign locked too early can be reopened with a new deadline (the people still expected are notified). While the campaign runs, the console lets you excuse a person (category + reason, optionally “until”) and chase latecomers by name. Closing finalises the lines with no open dispute, stamps “completed” only on the participants who genuinely finished, writes the shortfall to the log, and triggers IDP generation from the validated gaps.
Example, on invented people (run played on 2026-09-02): campaign 2026-Q3 is Locked, opened 01/06, due 31/08; 78 people enrolled for 3,340 expected skills. The console shows “0% approved · 76 not started · 0 in review · 78 people”. On a run played end to end: Souleymane LARSÈN rates “Procurement Support” 3 → submits (the review is born rating=NULL, gap=NULL, date=NULL) → his supervisor Fabrice BECKER opens the review → requests changes → Souleymane resubmits → the supervisor approves at 2: with no reason the save is refused (“Please give a short reason for rating this skill differently”); with the reason, the review becomes completed, decision=approve, rating=2, gap=−1 and the official level moves to 2, historised as supervisor_review. The six “UAT” campaigns that were never opened all read “Not launched — no participant enrolled”: the absence of measurement is named there, not converted into 0%.
Good practices
  • Actually open the campaign: while it is a draft, nobody is enrolled and no reminder goes out.
  • Supervisors: clear the queue in days. A review nobody opened shows no rating and no gap — that is deliberate, and it is visible.
  • Ask for a written justification in the NOTES column on high ratings; reviews become fast and disputes rare.
  • A rating that differs from the employee’s must be explained — the field is mandatory, do not dodge it with a complacent identical rating.
  • Never exclude SKILLS to lighten a campaign: the only exclusion designed in applies to a PERSON, with a reason.

Joiner / Mover / Leaver (JML) — and what a revert does, or does not, undo

Who: Manager or Admin (declares the event) · The platform (runs the effects) · SuperAdmin only (revert)
When: On every arrival, transfer or departure. Three kinds only: joiner, mover, leaver.
  1. Joiner — the person is enrolled into the open campaign (if none is open the event stops there and says so), and one empty self-assessment line is created per skill of their role. Those lines are NULL, not 0: “not yet assessed” must never read as the rating “None”. The employee gets their welcome notification.
  2. Mover — assessment history is kept, and a handover plan opens automatically, due in 30 days.
  3. Leaver — the account is deactivated, every session is revoked, any linked admin account is deactivated and its API keys revoked (the exact ids are recorded on the event), a personal-data cleanup job is scheduled per the country’s legal delay, and a handover plan opens, due in 14 days, pre-seeded with 5 items: in-flight work, key contacts, systems & access, recurring duties, tacit knowledge. The manager is notified.
  4. The handover plan is driven item by item. It cannot be marked completed while an item is still open: the platform refuses and says how many remain. Without that rule a “completed” plan dropped out of the chase list and was never followed up again.
  5. Revert (SuperAdmin only, note mandatory): the event is never deleted, it is marked reverted, dated, signed, explained.
  6. What a revert DOES undo: joiner → deletes only the self-assessment lines still untouched, in the origin campaign; leaver → reactivates the employee, reactivates the admin accounts and API keys whose ids the event recorded, deletes the not-yet-run data cleanup; joiner and leaver → cancels the handover plan it created, unless it is already completed.
  7. What a revert does NOT undo: the campaign enrolment stays; handover items are not deleted; revoked sessions do not come back; notifications already sent stay; the audit trail stays; the event stays marked “processed”; and reactivation adds a new movement inactive→active — history grows, it is not rewritten. A mover has nothing structural to undo: only its handover plan is cancelled.
Example, on invented people: a leaver event declared on Yao HALVORSEN (DEMO-3007).   • A revert is a data-entry correction, not the cancellation of an HR decision — it restores neither sessions nor messages already sent.

The individual development plan (IDP) — where it comes from, how it activates, how it closes

Who: The platform (generates) · Manager or Supervisor (fills in, signs) · Employee (executes, signs) · LMS (reports completions)
When: Three triggers: closing a campaign, a blue-zone 9-box, or a manual creation. One open plan per person at a time, across all campaigns.
  1. Generated at campaign close: the platform takes every requirement where the supervisor personally recorded a level below the required one — never a rejection, never an unvalidated self-rating — and creates a draft plan, one SMART objective per gap, one dated training action linked to the skill.
  2. Generated from the 9-box: an approved blue-zone placement (high potential / medium performance, or medium potential / high performance) proposes a 180-day plan pre-seeded with the role’s 5 largest gaps. If a plan is already open it is adopted — never duplicated.
  3. Honest objective wording: a gap on a skill never assessed is written “Reach level N (current level not assessed)”, not “from level 0 to level N”. The plan does not invent a starting point nobody measured.
  4. Attach the means: a catalogue course mapped to the skill is proposed automatically; a coaching plan can be opened, compulsorily anchored to this IDP.
  5. Dual sign-off: employee and supervisor each sign once (IP and browser timestamped). Once both signatures exist the plan moves from draft to active and both parties are notified.
  6. Closing an action with a post-rating: if it exceeds the known level, the skill rises and the gap closes at the next campaign.
  7. Cancellation: an IDP is not deleted. It is requested for cancellation with a reason, and a second administrator decides — the requester cannot approve their own request.
Example, on invented people: the manager approves a high potential / medium performance 9-box (“Shooting Star”, box 8) on Norah HARTLEY (DEMO-1115), role Data Platform Lead. The platform immediately creates IDP #12, draft, medium priority, 2026-09-02 → 2027-03-01, with 5 objectives drawn from the role’s largest gaps: “Develop ‘Security, Privacy & Compliance’ from level 1 to level 4”, “… Ways of Working & Operating Model Design … 1 to 4”, “… Adoption, Change & Stakeholder Management … 1 to 3”, “… Business Problem Framing & Value Orientation … 1 to 3”, “… Data Governance & Quality … 1 to 3”. Employee and supervisor are notified. The plan stays a draft until both signatures exist.
Good practices
  • One open IDP per person at a time, across all campaigns: close the old one before expecting a new one.
  • Two or three real objectives beat five wishes — the generator proposes five, prune it.
  • Date the actions and close them with a rating: “done” without measurement teaches nothing and moves no readiness figure.
  • An objective reading “current level not assessed” is not a display bug: it is an invitation to get the skill assessed first.

The improvement plan (PIP) — proposal, activation, closure, and what “success rate” actually means

Who: Manager (owns the whole procedure, no HR gate) · Employee · The platform (opens one itself on a red 9-box) · A second administrator (for any cancellation)
When: When performance stays below the bar despite coaching — or automatically as soon as a low-performance 9-box placement is approved.
  1. Propose: objectives, success criteria, review checkpoints, support offered, and above all a dated period — start and end. State is “proposed”. One open PIP per person: the platform refuses the duplicate rather than stacking two.
  2. Automatic opening: an approved 9-box placement at low performance (whatever the potential) opens a 90-day PIP and, with it, a coaching plan whose actions list the role’s critical gaps. The PIP text never names the grid cell — the placement stays confidential.
  3. Activate it and say so face to face. State becomes “active”: this is when the period actually starts running. A PIP never activated measured nothing.
  4. Support through the paired coaching plan: GROW sessions, progress notes, objectives carried from one session to the next.
  5. Close with an outcome: success or failure, plus a free-text note. The note explains, the state decides.
  6. Read the success rate honestly. The dashboard counts only the closures whose period actually ran — a declared end date, already reached at the moment of closing. A PIP opened and shut the day after over a three-month period is not a success: it is an administrative closure, and it is counted separately, under “not measured”. Until there are at least 4 measured closures, the dashboard shows “Not measured”, not a percentage.
  7. Cancelling a PIP goes through a reasoned request decided by a second administrator; the state becomes “cancelled”, nothing is deleted.
Observed example (demo dataset, 2026-09-02): four PIPs exist — #12 proposed (never activated), #13 failure closed 15/06 over a period ending 31/12, #14 success closed 15/06 over a period ending 31/07, #19 success closed 02/09 over a period ending 30/11. None of those four closures let its period run. The live aggregate therefore reads: 2 successes, 1 failure, but 0 measured closures — and the dashboard shows “Not measured”, not “67%”. A fifth PIP played as a probe (proposed 02/09, period to 01/12, activated, then closed as a success the same day) did not move the measured counter either: it stayed at 0.
Good practices
  • Set the end date, and respect it: without an end date that has been reached, the closure does not count — and that is what protects the statistic.
  • Activate the PIP. A “proposed” PIP that gets closed never measured anything, and the platform says so.
  • Criteria must be measurable and reachable inside the window: an impossible PIP is a dismissal letter in disguise.
  • Log every session: the documentation protects BOTH sides.
  • A “Not measured” success rate is not a bug: it is the refusal to publish a statistic based on three files shut the day they opened.

Coaching and mentoring — always anchored to something

Who: Manager, Supervisor or Admin within scope (opens, validates) · Named coach or mentor · Employee
When: Continuously. A coaching plan supports an IDP, a PIP, or one specific skill gap — never anything else.
  1. Pick the context, compulsorily: IDP, PIP, or skill gap. The platform refuses an unanchored plan (“A coaching/mentoring plan must be linked to a context”), and checks the chosen IDP or PIP really belongs to that person.
  2. Pick the nature: coaching or mentoring. Title, objective, expected outcome and a target date.
  3. Add actions — on a plan opened automatically by a red 9-box they are already written, one per critical gap of the role.
  4. Run the sessions in GROW format (Goal, Reality, Options, Way forward). Objectives still open at the end of a session are carried forward automatically to the next one, linked back to the original.
  5. Track progress 0 to 100%. The monitor separates active plans from overdue ones (target date passed).
  6. Validate completion: the plan becomes “completed”, 100%, with the validator’s name and date; the employee is notified. An already-completed plan cannot be re-validated.
  7. Several open plans are legitimate for one person — one per context. That is not a duplicate, and the platform does not block it.

  • An unanchored plan is refused — deliberately: coaching serves an IDP, a PIP or a named gap, otherwise it cannot be measured.
  • Set the target date: it is what feeds the monitor’s “overdue” list.
  • Let the automatic carry-forward work — an unmet objective comes back, it is not lost between two sessions.

The 9-box — proposal, approval, supersede-by-archive, disclosure, and the automatic follow-through

Who: Supervisor (drafts the proposal) · Manager or Admin within scope (approves, discloses) · Calibration panel (harmonises) · Employee (sees nothing before disclosure)
When: After each campaign, and at least every 3 months — the platform flags placements due for review by itself.
  1. Proposal: the supervisor rates performance and potential (low / medium / high); the platform computes the box (1 to 9) and its label. It can also suggest a position from measured readiness — and when assessment coverage is too thin it says so (“Not measured: 0 / N requirement(s) assessed”) and never drops to the lower tier, because that tier is what triggers a PIP.
  2. One open proposal per person: re-assessing updates the existing proposal, it does not create a second one.
  3. Submit then approve. On approval, any earlier approved placement is archived — that is supersede-by-archive: nothing is erased, the old one becomes “archived” with a note of what replaced it, and exactly one approved placement survives per person.
  4. Approval is the trigger point: high potential/medium performance or medium potential/high performance → 180-day IDP. Low performance → the software opens no plan (arbitration A3): a task is raised for the hierarchical manager, who opens the improvement plan with a written reason — and can withdraw it themselves, with no two-person procedure. The “red zone” is ALL low performance, whatever the potential — three cells, not just the low/low corner; and the top-right corner (“Gold Star”) triggers nothing: it is a recognition cell, not an action cell. One open task per person, and no task at all if a plan is already open.
  5. Disclosure: the placement is confidential by default. Until it is explicitly disclosed the employee cannot see it — and only an approved placement can be disclosed. Disclosing it, and retracting it, both require a written reason; the placement keeps who disclosed it and when, and the person sees those three things beside their cell. No text they can read names the cell until it has been disclosed. The disclosure notification carries no value at all, only the link.
  6. Calibration: in session, every move requires a rationale — the platform refuses an empty one. Finalising writes the new placement into both reading systems (the grid and the talent record) so they cannot diverge. A session with no adjustment at all is not finalised: it is returned as-is, “no adjustments”, rather than stamped “finalised”.
  7. Bias scan: a sweep compares each site and department against the mean. Below 5 placements overall or 3 per group, no alert is produced — the sample is declared too small, not “clean”.
Example, on invented people (run played on 13/09/2026): on Norah HARTLEY (DEMO-1115), a low/low draft — box 1, source “manager” — is submitted then approved. The grid log records create, submit, approve. On approval no plan is created: the platform raises one task for the hierarchical manager (employee 201) and notifies her, and writes the talent mirror box “low-low”, source “override”. The manager then opens PIP #15 with her written reason, and coaching plan #11 is attached to that plan. The placement stays undisclosed: measured over HTTP, the employee’s “My development” page contains the cell name nowhere. After a reasoned disclosure by uat.admin, the same page shows “Point de vigilance — disclosed by uat.admin on 13/09/2026”, with the reason. Replayed as medium/high (box 8), it is an IDP that opens. On a test instance the state of the 37 placements tells the story: 23 archived, 11 drafts, 3 approved — archiving really is the supersede mechanism. On the 9-box roster, people never placed read “— not assessed — ⏰ Due”, never filed into a default box.
Good practices
  • Calibrate before disclosing: a written rationale makes the placement defensible.
  • Disclose in person, one to one. The grid starts a conversation, it does not hand down a verdict.
  • Never disclose a proposal: only an approved placement can be, and the platform refuses otherwise.
  • A “not measured” suggestion means assess first, then place — not place on a hunch.
  • Run the bias scan every campaign — and read “sample too small” as “we do not know”, not as “nothing to report”.

Continuity — critical roles, succession benches, risk of loss and handovers

Who: Manager or Admin with “Manage succession” (their scope) · Admin with “Manage retention risk” · The scheduler (recomputes, chases)
When: Every plan is reviewed every 6 months — the platform asks for it itself — and immediately on any departure from a critical role.
  1. Mark the critical roles: a criticality score 1 to 5, a vacancy risk (low / medium / high), a business impact, a time to fill. Vacancy risk is not calculated: it is a human judgement, entered and owned. A role counts as critical from score 4 upwards — merely carrying a criticality record is not enough.
  2. Create a succession plan per critical role — one non-archived plan per role. The platform sets the next review at 6 months by itself.
  3. Seed the bench: the platform proposes the best candidates by computing their readiness against the target role’s framework, with a floor. The banding follows: ≥ 90% → ready now, ≥ 70% → 1–2 years, otherwise 3+ years. The “emergency” band is never assigned automatically — it is a human designation, as is the emergency cover named separately on the plan.
  4. A lapsed certificate drops the effective level to 0 in that readiness computation: nobody is banded “ready” while their ticket is no longer valid.
  5. Risk of loss: the platform recomputes a score out of 100 every night = impact of loss (up to 50, from the 9-box placement) + flight risk (up to 50: high potential with no active IDP, a live PIP, morale falling in the surveys). A manager may force the bands by hand; the score keeps being recomputed for audit.
  6. Strict confidentiality of risk of loss: nobody sees their own record — the query excludes them explicitly, and recomputing or overriding one’s own is refused. The 9-box key is stripped from the factors returned to a manager, because that page spans their whole sub-tree while the 9-box is only disclosed person by person. The manager is told only on a crossing into “high”, once, and only as a count.
  7. Handover: opened automatically on every mover (30 days) or leaver (14 days), with its 5 items. The chase list only asks for what is overdue, due soon with open items, or dormant for 30 days with no date — a freshly created plan is deliberately not chased.
  8. Review cadence: every week the platform sends one aggregated notification per recipient — plans past their review date and critical roles with no bench, as counts, naming nobody.
Example, on invented people: the leaver event on Yao HALVORSEN opens handover plan #4, due 2026-09-16, owner admin #1, with its five items (“In-flight work & deadlines”, “Key contacts & stakeholders”, “Systems, accounts & access”, “Recurring duties & cadence”, “Tacit knowledge & gotchas”). On a test instance 78 retention-risk records are computed, while 0 succession plans and 0 roles marked critical exist: the module is waiting to be seeded, and Continuity’s “Get started” panel says so rather than showing 100% coverage. The five existing handover plans are four open and one completed, the latter with a named incoming person and a 31/12 due date.
Good practices
  • A role is critical only from score 4 up — below that it appears in no bench alert.
  • Every critical role has at least one “ready now” OR a named emergency cover. “We’ll figure it out” is not a plan.
  • Risk of loss is confidential by design — nobody sees their own. Hold the same line in conversation.
  • Start the handover the day the departure is known; the plan closes only once every item is settled.
  • An empty bench reads as an empty bench: the platform counts it and chases it, it does not fill it in for you.

Certification and compliance — validity, revalidation window, lapse, position coverage

Who: Admin with “Manage compliance” (policies, rules) · Manager (team chasing) · Employee (files their ticket) · The scheduler (alerts, predicts)
When: Policy set once per certifying skill. After that everything is automatic: alerts at 90, 60, 30 days and on expiry.
  1. Declare the policy on the skill: validity in months (empty = never expires), revalidation window in days (default 90), and an optional decay period.
  2. Record a certificate: number, issue date, virus-scanned attachment. The expiry is computed from the policy when not supplied, clamping to the last day of the month rather than overflowing. An issue date in the future is refused — without that rule a post-dated ticket became “the current certificate” and hid a real lapse.
  3. Four states, and no more: valid · expiring (inside the revalidation window — still valid today, that is a runway, not a loss of qualification) · expired · no expiry. Somebody who never held a ticket has no state at all: they are absent from the list, they are not “expired”.
  4. Lapse: somebody who held a certificate and no longer does. Their effective level drops to 0 for that skill in readiness, benchmark and succession — but the assessment record stays: what moves is the verdict “is this person qualified today”, not the measurement. The cause is distinguished: expired or revoked.
  5. Revocation notifies the person. Before, one stopped being qualified in silence.
  6. Position coverage rules (“safe-shift”): a scope, a skill, a minimum level and a minimum qualified headcount. The minimum level is bounded to the real 1–4 scale — a “≥ 5” rule was unsatisfiable, hence a permanent alert. A rule may require a valid certificate, and that switch decides whether a lapsed holder still counts: without it they do; with it they are excluded.
  7. Prediction: over 14 days the platform projects planned absences and certificate expiries, and flags a rule that will fall below its minimum, with the date.
  8. Alert once per transition, never once per tick, and beyond 25 breached rules a single batched message goes out — the cap limits the paging, never the record.
Observed example (2026-09-02): the “Budget & Cost Control” policy is: 12 months validity, 90-day revalidation window, 6-month decay. The only certificate on file — no. VOC-TEST-001, issued 31/08/2025, expiring 14/09/2026 — is “expiring”, 12 days out: it is still valid, and the Compliance screen shows exactly “EXPIRING (IN WINDOW) 1”, with “VALID 0”, “EXPIRED 0” and “LAPSED SKILL LEVELS 0”. The lapsed list is empty — nobody has lost a qualification. The rule “Demo — coverage: Budget & Cost Control (IT)” requires ≥ 1 person at level ≥ 1, with no valid-certificate requirement: it counts 5 qualified and reads COVERED, with no predicted breach.
Good practices
  • “Expiring” means “still valid, renew now” — do not read it as a breach.
  • No certificate on file is not the same as an expired one: the first degrades nothing, the second drops the effective level to 0.
  • Tick “valid certificate required” on the rules the law demands it for — that switch, and only that switch, excludes a lapsed holder from the count.
  • Do not bulk-generate the rule grid without the preview: it states how many rules would be in breach the moment they exist.
  • An issue date is never entered in the future; the platform refuses it, and that is what protects the compliance reading.

Contesting a review — the L0 → L1 → L2 ladder and the SLA that stops it blocking

Who: Employee (opens) · Supervisor (L0) · Manager (L1) · Local admin with “Arbitrate disputes” — the HR arbitration (L2) · The scheduler (escalates every 15 minutes)
When: As soon as an employee disagrees with the recorded rating. Three SLAs, configurable in Settings → Disputes.
  1. Open the dispute with a reason. Immediate effect: the self-assessment line goes back to provisional, the review is marked disputed, and the supervisor who rated is notified.
  2. An open dispute stops the close from finalising that line — which is exactly why the ladder is timed: with no SLA, one forgotten dispute would block the campaign indefinitely.
  3. L0 — the supervisor decides within 5 days (default). They set the decided rating and explain; the reason is mandatory.
  4. Automatic escalation to L1 past that SLA: the dispute becomes “escalated”, and the employee’s manager is notified. They have 7 days (default), counted from the escalation.
  5. Automatic escalation to L2 past that second SLA: HR arbitration — a local admin holding the “Arbitrate disputes” permission. They are notified once per batch, with a count, never once per file. They have 7 days (default).
  6. Automatic finalisation if the arbitration never happened: the dispute becomes “auto-finalised”, on the supervisor’s rating, with “[auto-finalized: no HR decision within SLA]” appended to the reason, the review is marked completed, the line is finalised, and the employee is told their contested rating is now official. All of it is logged.
  7. At every level, the decision writes the decided rating into the official profile and lifts the line’s lock, so the campaign can close.
Example, on invented people: after the approval at 2 of “Procurement Support” for Souleymane LARSÈN, he opens a dispute. Immediately: the dispute is born L0 / open, the self-assessment reverts to provisional and the review becomes disputed. Past the L0 SLA, automatic escalation moves it to L1 / escalated; past the L1 SLA, to L2 / escalated. Both escalations execute and each returns 1 file handled. The L2 automatic finalisation used to fail up to this release — it wrote a null author into a NOT NULL column, leaving the dispute stuck at “L2 / escalated” — ; that defect is fixed: promoting the rating now attributes the write to a system account, exactly as the normal review path already did. On a test instance there is currently no dispute at all (0 rows): the open-disputes screen is empty, which is a state, not a result of zero contestation.
Good practices
  • Settle at the lowest level: a dispute resolved by the supervisor in 48 h costs less than an HR arbitration three weeks later.
  • The decision reason is mandatory at every level — it is what makes the decision defensible.
  • Set the three SLAs before opening a campaign, not during it.
  • An escalation does not mean somebody did a bad job: it means time passed. That is what the SLA is for.

Delegating administration — scope and capability are granted separately

Who: SuperAdmin (delegates, revokes, attests) · Admin with “Manage admins” (sees the delegation) · The scheduler (chases the review monthly)
When: At go-live, then reviewed quarterly and on every role change.
  1. Understand the two halves. Scope answers “over WHOM?” (site, department, service, country, region). Capability answers “to DO WHAT?” (the permission catalogue). They are granted separately, and either alone does nothing: an account with a scope and zero permissions is a powerless account; a permission with no scope reaches nobody.
  2. Grant through a ready-made access profile (site HR, department lead…) which ticks the right boxes, or permission by permission. A permission can carry an expiry; an unreadable or already-past date makes the whole change refused, because “empty” means “permanent” and a typo used to mint an eternal grant.
  3. The guardrails work by themselves: a delegate can only grant what they hold; they cannot step outside their own scope; a viewer can never exercise a write permission even if one is stored for them; and above all a delegate may administer an account only if that account governs nobody they do not already govern. In doubt, the rule refuses.
  4. Everything is recorded in an access ledger: grant, revoke, scope added or removed, profile applied, expiry extended. That ledger is evidence, not permission — authorisation stays in the permissions and the scopes.
  5. The access review names four exceptions per account: scoped but powerless, no MFA, grant expiring within 30 days, account idle for 90 days; plus profile drift (“provisioned as X, no longer looks like X”).
  6. Attest account by account: “appropriate” or “to revoke”. The attestation goes into the hash-chained, append-only log.
  7. The Delegation view answers the reverse question: for this site, this country, who has authority over my people? — and flags a populated unit nobody covers.
  8. Deactivate, never delete. “Deactivate” (reason required) keeps the account’s capabilities and scopes — marked “revoked” — closes its sessions and API keys, and “Reactivate” (reason, password change forced) restores exactly the previous perimeter. An account whose grants have expired reads “Expired on …”, never “never provisioned”; an account locked after too many failed sign-ins is unlocked from the list. The Delegation view is reachable from Administrators → Delegation of authority (/admin/delegation).
  9. Some sensitive operations go through a second pair of eyes (maker-checker): the submitter can never approve their own request.
Observed example (2026-09-02): of the 13 active admin accounts, ten local administrators hold a live scope but ZERO live capability — ops.local (2 scopes, 0 permissions), hse.local (1, 0), maint.local (1, 0), proc.local (2, 0), fin.local (1, 0), hr.local (2, 0), it.local (2, 0), geo.local (1, 0), lab.local (1, 0). Only test.local actually carries rights: 18 live capabilities over 1 scope. That is precisely the “scoped but powerless” exception the access review names, and it is the most common defect in this family. The access ledger holds 0 rows: it has nothing to tell because those grants predate it — the Delegation view therefore shows the origin as “unknown” rather than inventing one.
Good practices
  • Give both halves or neither: a scope with no permission creates an administrator who can do nothing, and believes otherwise.
  • Time-bound the grants: access should expire unless renewed, not persist unless revoked.
  • Never share a SuperAdmin account — delegate the exact permission, in the exact scope.
  • Run the review quarterly and attest: “scoped but powerless”, “no MFA” and “idle 90 days” are the first three lines to clear.
  • The access ledger is audit evidence, not a source of authority — never read it to decide who may do what.

Being told without being flooded — in-app first, email second, and never twice

Who: Everyone (receives) · Each person (sets their quiet hours and opt-out) · Admin (sets the email families) · The scheduler (chases, groups)
When: Continuously. Daily, weekly or monthly reminders depending on the kind.
  1. In-app first, always. Every event drops a notification in the bell. Email is only a second channel, and it is filtered: some families never go by email, others wait for the daily rollup, only the most urgent go immediately.
  2. The bell adds two counters: the actions genuinely waiting for you, and unread notifications. The badge is the sum, capped at 99+.
  3. Quiet hours: each person sets a window. A notification raised inside it is deferred, not lost — it resurfaces at the end of the window, and it resurfaces even if the scheduler never ran. An urgent email raised inside the window is composed immediately and parked, so it is not downgraded hours later.

  5. Reminders do not repeat. Every chase is claimed before it is sent in a ledger, by period (day, week, month): a crash cannot produce a duplicate, and a failed send releases the claim to be retried next time.
  6. Talent alerts are aggregated and anonymous: “3 plans to review”, never “X’s plan”. Retention risk and 9-box placements stay inside the application, never reaching a webhook.
  7. Four rollups: the weekly manager digest (Monday), the subscription-based departmental digest, the daily personal digest of your unread items, and the monthly planning brief. A recipient with nothing to read gets nothing.
  8. The manager digest separates measured from unmeasured: observed critical gaps are red, critical skills not yet assessed are listed separately, in amber. The departmental digest shows “not measured” rather than 0 when readiness has no base.
Observed example (demo dataset): 215 notifications, of which 156 campaign reminders, 11 manager digests, 10 “self-assessment approved”, 6 “coaching plan created”, 4 “access review”, 3 “talent plan due”, 3 “handover due”, 3 “campaign escalation”, 2 “certificate expiry” and 2 “certificate expiry — team”, 2 “IDP created”, 2 “PIP created”, 2 “leaver”, 1 “coverage breached”, 1 “coverage — predicted breach”. All on the in-app channel. The reminder ledger holds 167 claims: 153 “campaign not started” (one per person per week), 4 “access review” (monthly), 3 “talent plan due”, 3 “handover due”, 2 “planning brief”, 2 “campaign auto-lock” — last written 2026-09-02.
Good practices
  • Set your quiet hours rather than opting out: what is deferred comes back, what is opted out does not.
  • Do not look for the detail in the notification: it is deliberately not there. Follow the link.
  • Check the email families after any SMTP change — a disabled family never disables the bell.
  • A digest that does not arrive because there was nothing to say is a digest that works.

Getting data in and out — templates, preview, round trip, snapshots, SQL console

Who: SuperAdmin or admin with “Import” / “Export” data (the SQL console and snapshots are SuperAdmin only)
When: At go-live, then on every change to the organisation or the framework.
  1. Start from the template, never an improvised file: organisation, domains and skills, roles, employees, assessments, full framework, certifications, local admins — in Excel or JSON, with a “full system” template pre-filled with an example. Every download is logged.
  2. Preview is mandatory before any import. The Excel preview validates the structure accepting the same sheet names the importer accepts. The JSON preview is a true dry run that writes nothing: it states, per entity, how many rows are new and how many already exist — and how many logins would be minted.
  3. The round trip is faithful. In the role matrix a cell carries two facts: the required level, and a star marking the requirement critical (“3*”). Without it an Excel round trip silently cleared the critical flag on every requirement. Level 0 is a valid rating and survives the round trip; an empty cell means “not assessed”, which is different.
  4. Import merges, it never truncates: it creates or updates, and reports both. An employee whose site, department, service or role cannot be resolved is skipped with a named error — not half inserted.
  5. Snapshot before any risky manoeuvre. A snapshot captures the organisation, the framework, roles and requirements, employees (without passwords), assessments and their history, admins and scopes, and settings. It does not capture PIPs, IDPs, coaching, 9-box, disputes, lifecycle events, notifications or the log.
  6. Restoring a snapshot requires a full backup first; if that fails the restore is aborted and says so. The restore clears what the snapshot does not cover rather than leaving orphan rows: it is a reset to the snapshot’s state, and the manual must say so.
  7. SQL console (SuperAdmin): it owns the transaction, refuses any statement that would touch the three append-only tables (assessment history, system log, review signatures) — including one disguised inside a dynamic block — and takes a full restore point before any write. If the restore point fails, the script does not run.
  8. Console revert: the restore preserves the three protected tables, puts them back with their hash chain intact, and only frees the quarantine area once every row is back; otherwise they are named and kept.
  9. Restore points are unencrypted copies of all personal data: they are therefore capped in number and purged beyond 7 days.
Observed example: the database holds 4 snapshots. The Data Management screen does expose template downloads, preview, import, snapshots and the SQL console, on a SuperAdmin account only. Not executed by this lane: no import, no restore and no SQL-console script was run — those operations write outside a transaction (pg_dump backup, restore, purge) and cannot be rolled back cleanly. They are documented from the code and schema.
Good practices
  • Never import without a preview; never bulk-change without a snapshot.
  • Keep the star in the role matrix: without it the round trip clears the critical flag on your requirements.
  • An empty cell is not a zero. Do not fill the blanks to “tidy up”: you would turn an absence of measurement into a result.
  • A snapshot is not a backup: it contains no PIP, no IDP, no coaching, no 9-box and no log.
  • SQL-console restore points are complete unencrypted copies: treat them as such, and let the 7-day purge do its job.

What runs while nobody is watching — backups, pruning and the job calendar

Who: The platform (runs it) · SuperAdmin (sets hours and retention) · Operations (watches /about)
When: Every 15 minutes, hourly, daily, weekly, monthly depending on the job.
  1. One scheduler at a time. On a simple install the jobs run inside the application process, protected by a lock: if two instances start, only one runs the calendar.
  2. Daily backup: the first hourly run at or after the configured hour produces a compressed pg_dump, named by date — hence idempotent: a second run the same day does nothing. The password goes through the environment, never the command line. A partial file is deleted so it cannot look like a success. The date and status of the last backup are published on the About page.
  3. Backup retention: the newest N are kept. 0 means “keep everything”, and the value is floored — a fractional value used to wipe the entire set.
  4. Telemetry pruning — and only telemetry: performance traces (30 days), already-read notifications (120 days), the reminder ledger (180 days). Again 0 = keep forever. The system log is never pruned: it is a hash-chained audit artefact.
  5. Automatic campaign locking at the deadline (on by default). Automatic closing is off by default: it only turns on by giving a positive grace period, because closing a campaign is a decision, not a consequence of the calendar.
  6. Chases never duplicate: every job claims its send in the ledger before emitting it, and releases the claim if the send fails.
  7. Everything that alerts, alerts only on a change: a coverage rule warns when it flips, not every tick; a certificate warns once per stage (90, 60, 30, expired).
Observed example (demo dataset): the KPI snapshots were written on every active day — 22, 24, 25, 26, 27, 28 August then 1 and 2 September, 10 rows per day (the organisation plus each site). The 2026-09-02 picture reads: 79 people, 73 measured, average readiness 82.3% over the measured against 73.9% over everyone, assessment coverage 73.8% (2,476 requirements assessed out of 3,353), 23 roles at risk, 181 sole holders, 147 roles with nobody qualified, 1 certificate expiring within 90 days. Both averages are published side by side: that is how “82% of the measured” is never mistaken for “82% of the people”. Not executed: no job was triggered manually by this lane.
Good practices
  • Check the date and status of the last backup on the About page, not the presence of a file.
  • Never put a fractional value in a retention setting: 0 means “keep everything”, and nothing else means “delete everything”.
  • Only enable automatic campaign closing deliberately — it generates the IDPs.
  • Do not try to prune the system log: it is append-only by design, and it is your evidence.

What changed, and how to correct it cleanly — movements, post-approval revisions, cancellations

Who: The database (captures movements by itself) · Manager or Admin (contests an approved rating, requests a cancellation) · A SECOND administrator (decides)
When: Continuously for movements; on demand for the two correction procedures.
  1. Movements capture themselves, at database level: seven watched dimensions — site, department, service, role, manager, supervisor, status. One row per dimension that actually changed, with the label resolved at the moment of the change: history is not rewritten when a service is renamed later.
  2. The movement feed merges three streams: structural changes, skill assessments, and reviews. It is filtered by the reader’s scope before any aggregation.
  3. Post-approval revision: when an approved rating is plainly wrong, it is not rewritten in place. A reasoned request is filed carrying the approved level and the proposed level. One live request per self-assessment. An administrator decides; the database itself forbids a decision with no decider and no date. Approved, the request updates the review’s rating.
  4. Cancelling a plan (coaching, mentoring, PIP, IDP): never a deletion. A reasoned request, the prior state captured, and a second administrator deciding — the requester cannot approve their own request. Approved, the plan simply becomes “cancelled”.
  5. A plan already completed, closed or archived cannot be cancelled: the platform answers “already closed”.
Observed example (demo dataset): 16 movements recorded. Twelve are service changes on 04/08 — for instance “Data & Insights (IT / Riverside) → Enterprise Applications (IT / Riverside)” then back, each label qualified by its department and site. The last four concern the leaver record ERASED-90010: a manager change “FARRELL, Aïcha → BECKER, Fabrice” on 01/09, then status active → inactive, inactive → active on 01/09, and active → inactive again on 02/09. History grows every time, it is never corrected in place. To date, 0 post-approval revisions and 0 cancellation requests exist: both queues are empty.
Good practices
  • Never correct an approved rating in place: file a post-approval revision, it leaves a decided, dated trail.
  • A cancellation requires a second pair of eyes — that is the rule, do not work around it by deleting.
  • Read the movement feed before concluding data has “disappeared”: more often it was moved, and the feed says so.
  • Two empty queues do not mean “no errors”: they mean no correction procedure is currently running.

Glossary

Not measured (“—”)The single most important idea in this product: an absence of measurement is not a result. A skill nobody has assessed shows “—” and “Not measured”; it is never rounded to 0 and never counted as a gap. A level 0 is the opposite — somebody looked and found nothing. Both appear side by side on the same dashboard: “Training & Mentorship” required 1 / current 0 / gap −1 (a measured zero, counted as a gap) against “Budget & Cost Control” required 1 / current — / Not measured (never assessed, not counted). That is why the counter is split: “9 skill gaps · 0 critical · 1 not measured”. The arithmetic closes: 39 met + 9 measured gaps + 1 unmeasured = 49, and 39/49 = 89%.
Skill level 0–4The rating scale actually shown on screen: 0 None · 1 Basic awareness · 2 Guided · 3 Autonomous · 4 Expert (the dashboard footer spells the same scale out as “0 No knowledge · 1 Basic awareness · 2 Guided performance · 3 Autonomous · 4 Expert / can teach”). 2 “Guided” means you need someone with you; 3 “Autonomous” means you do not — that boundary is the one most decisions turn on. Until a level is chosen, the selector reads “— Choose —” and the skill counts as not measured, not as a 0.
Skill gapA measured shortfall: the level a role requires minus the level actually observed. A requirement that was never assessed produces no gap at all — it is reported separately as “not measured”. “Go and measure” and “go and train” are two different instructions, and the product refuses to merge them.
SupervisorWhoever is named in an employee’s “supervisor” column. They instruct the file: open the review, comment, send it back, enter their own rating, approve or reject; they draft 9-Box placements and run coaching. They cannot manager-validate a reviewed file, arbitrate a disagreement, or approve / reject / archive / disclose a 9-Box placement — all six answer HTTP 403 “Not authorized: manager/admin only”, measured, with the data strictly unchanged afterwards.
ManagerWhoever is named in an employee’s “manager” column (manager type “employee”). A manager holds everything a supervisor holds, plus four powers that are theirs alone: validate a file already reviewed by the supervisor, arbitrate a disagreement, and approve / reject / archive / disclose a 9-Box placement. The relationship is strictly one-way: no supervisor action is closed to a manager. Their span contains their supervisors’ — on the demonstration data, 16 people against 15, the supervisor herself included.
Supervisor vs Manager — the difference at a glanceBoth sign in with the same “Manager” badge and exactly the same 39-entry menu, so the difference is invisible on screen and only appears when acting.

Both can: receive and open reviews · request changes (comment mandatory) · approve or reject a skill · enter their own rating with a mandatory gap reason · run coaching, development plans, PIPs and gap analysis · draft and submit a 9-Box placement · resolve an L0/L1 dispute within their span.
Manager only: 9-Box approve / reject / archive / disclose · manager validation of a reviewed file · arbitration of a disagreement.
Neither: HR arbitration at L2 (an administrator holding “Arbitrate disputes”) · approving their own file.

A self-assessment goes first to the supervisor; with none set it goes to the manager; with neither it falls into the relevant administrator’s queue.
Span of controlEveryone an account can see and act on: their full sub-tree, reached through the supervision OR the management relationship. Outside that span every action is refused with 403, whatever the role — and forms follow the same rule, so forcing an id into a URL changes nothing.
Readiness SCOREA percentage: points obtained over the requirements that were actually assessed. When nothing has been assessed it reads “Not measured”, never 0 %. It answers “how good is what we measured?”.
Role-ready VERDICTA yes/no, and a different question from the score: READY requires readiness computed over ALL requirements (each unassessed one counting as a 0) to reach the threshold (80 % by default) AND every critical skill to be met. That is why a row can honestly read “100 % — 2 / 13 — NOT READY”: 100 % of what was measured, with eleven requirements never measured. The score judges the measurement; the verdict judges the person against the whole job.
CoverageThe share of the requirements in play that have actually been assessed. It is printed next to every score for one reason: a low figure at low coverage means “go and assess these people”, not “these people are weak”.
Required levelThe 0–4 proficiency a role demands for a given skill, optionally flagged critical. It is the target a current level is compared against. A requirement set to 0 stays in the list to assess but can create neither a gap nor an achievement — which is why one role can list 50 skills to rate while readiness is computed over 49.
Pillar (Domain)The top level of the capability framework. There are 6: 1. HSE & Operational Risk · 2. Functional Technical · 3. Digital, Data & Work Tools · 4. Compliance & Certification · 5. Business Acumen · 6. People Management.
Sub-Domain / Competency ElementThe middle level — a competency element inside a pillar, with a one-line definition. There are 49 (8–9 per pillar).
Capability Item (Skill)The most granular level — an individual skill hanging off a sub-domain, and what people rate 0–4. The About page reports the live counts of the loaded framework (on a sample instance: 6 pillars, 49 sub-domains, 56 role families, 1 126 skills).
Role FamilyA grouping that skills map to. A role can be created from a role family, which pre-suggests that family’s skills instead of starting from a blank list.
Department-designed skill countThe number of skills a role carries is designed by the department that owns the role, and the platform states on the campaign console that it is kept in full and never reduced. The only exclusion the product offers applies to a person, with a reason — never to a skill.
Self-assessmentWhen you rate your own skills, before a reviewer looks at them. A row you never touched stays empty (“— Choose —”, status NOT STARTED); it is not a 0.
Reviewer ratingThe 0–4 rating the reviewer enters themselves in the review console, pre-filled with the employee’s self-rating. That rating — not the self-rating — becomes the official level on approval. If it differs from the self-rating, a short written reason is mandatory and the save is refused without it.
RejectionA reviewer decision that requires a reason and produces no rating at all: the skill stays unmeasured, and no IDP or PIP is triggered. A rejection is not an agreement.
Assessment campaign (cycle)A timed window with four states: Draft → Open → Locked → Closed. Only opening enrols participants and builds the roster; a draft campaign has nobody in it and sends no reminder, and the console says exactly that: “Not launched — no participant enrolled”.
RosterThe list of people enrolled when a campaign is opened, each with the full department-designed count of skills expected, frozen at launch. It is what campaign progress is measured against.
Dispute ladder (L0 → L1 → L2)A timed escalation so no contested rating can stall a campaign: L0 the supervisor (5 days by default), L1 the manager (7 days), L2 HR arbitration — a local admin holding the “Arbitrate disputes” capability (7 days) — then automatic finalisation on the supervisor’s rating, logged and notified to the employee. The SLAs are set in Settings → Disputes.
9-BoxA grid of Performance × Potential with nine cells, used to calibrate talent. Placements are confidential by default and each person appears exactly once — the chip shown is always the approved placement; a ◌ marks a proposal with no approval behind it, a • an approved position with a newer draft awaiting a decision.
Red zone / blue zoneWhat approving a placement triggers. The red zone is ALL low performance, whatever the potential — three cells, not just the low/low corner — and it opens a 90-day PIP plus a coaching plan. The blue zone is high potential with medium performance, or medium potential with high performance, and it opens a 180-day IDP. The top-right corner (“Gold Star”) triggers nothing: it is a recognition cell, not an action cell. No duplicate is created if a plan is already open.
9-Box disclosureA manager’s explicit choice to let an employee see their own placement. Off by default, and only an approved placement can be disclosed — never a proposal. The disclosure notification carries no value, only a link.
Supersede-by-archiveApproving a new 9-box placement archives the previous approved one rather than deleting it: nothing is erased, the old row is marked archived with what replaced it, and exactly one approved placement survives per person.
PIPPerformance Improvement Plan — a support structure with a deadline for someone persistently below the bar. Propose → activate (this is when the period starts running) → close with an outcome. One open PIP per person. The success rate counts only closures whose period actually ran, and below four measured closures it reads “Not measured” rather than a flattering percentage.
IDPIndividual Development Plan — grows a person against named skills and target levels. One open IDP per person, all campaigns combined. Objectives are seeded from validated gaps; a skill that was never assessed produces an objective worded “current level not assessed”, not “from level 0”.
Coaching contextEvery coaching or mentoring plan must be anchored to an IDP, a PIP or a specific skill gap. The platform refuses a context-free plan — coaching always targets a named need.
Capability (permission)One named thing an account may do. The catalogue holds 31 capabilities across 6 areas (people & assessments 9, configuration 7, data 2, governance 4, continuity & learning 7, operational compliance 2), and every configuration area is split read / write — “View roles” can be granted without “Manage roles”. Your own page, My access (/mon-acces), lists all 31 with GRANTED / NOT GRANTED against your account.
Scope (perimeter)Who an account may act on: a country, site, department or service. Capability and scope are granted separately and you need both — a capability with no scope acts on nobody, and a scope with no capability does nothing at all. on a sample instance ten local admins held a perimeter and zero capability, and the Admins page says so in a banner.
Delegated-access expiryDelegated administration has a duration. The create form offers “Until” (default 12 months) or “Permanent access”, with the reminder that permanent access should stay the exception. An unreadable or lapsed date fails closed — the access stops rather than being assumed valid.
Access ReviewA super-admin screen (/admin/access-review) listing every admin account with its role, scope, capabilities, expiry, MFA state, last activity and the exceptions detected in plain words (“Perimeter without capability”, “No MFA”, “Dormant account”). Each row can be attested, and the attestation is written to the tamper-evident audit log. CSV export included.
Access ledgerThe record of every grant, revoke, scope change, profile application and expiry extension. It is evidence, not permission: authority lives in the capabilities and scopes, never in the ledger.
Scoped audit logThe system log is already partitioned by scope: on a sample instance the super admin saw 4 895 entries and a site-scoped local admin 158. Granting “View system logs” is therefore not the same as opening the whole organisation’s audit. Refusals are logged by name, which makes the log the clearest proof of where a boundary actually sits.
Import countersEvery import reports what happened to each row, not a flattering total: created (did not exist, inserted) · updated (existed with other values, rewritten) · unchanged (already matched, nothing written) · skipped (not applied, reason in the errors) · not rated (cells deliberately left blank in the template). A blank cell is never a level 0. Re-importing the application’s own export produced 1 116 skills unchanged, 1 932 requirements unchanged and 872 not rated — with zero rows created.
Password ruleAt least 12 characters with uppercase, lowercase, number and special character — announced under the field and enforced by the server everywhere a password is set. Length alone is not enough: common words, keyboard patterns and sequences are refused even beyond 12 characters. Changing your password signs out all your other sessions.
Active sessionA device or browser currently signed in as you. Every employee — not only administrators — can list their own at /account/sessions and sign the others out in one click. Separately, a super admin has a platform-wide session monitor.
Quiet hoursA personal window (both times, or neither) during which nothing appears in your bell and no email is sent. Nothing is dropped — everything is delivered when the window ends.
Two-factor auth (2FA/MFA)A second sign-in step — a 6-digit code from an authenticator app — plus 10 single-use backup codes. Optional for everyone by default; an administrator can force it on an account through its authentication policy.
Single sign-on (SSO)Signing in with your organisation’s identity provider (Microsoft Entra ID, generic OIDC, SAML 2.0 or Google Workspace). It maps an external identity to an existing account by email — there is no auto-provisioning unless self-service onboarding is also on. Note: granting someone administrator access creates a SEPARATE admin account that signs in with a password plus two-factor authentication, not by SSO; their SSO identity stays on their employee account. on a sample instance all four providers were inactive, so the end-to-end sign-in was not exercised.
Self-service onboardingPeople register themselves (open signup or SSO) and wait in a queue until an administrator places them into a site, department, service and role — that placement is what creates their account. Off by default on a fresh installation, and refused by default when no email domain is allow-listed.
BenchmarkThe set of required levels each role demands, skill by skill — role data, not people data. The Benchmark view lines roles up side by side against skills grouped by pillar → sub-domain (the Δ column is the spread between the most and least demanding role) and, below, measures how the current occupants fit. In the French interface the menu entry is labelled « Référentiel ».
Benchmark FitFor a role, how well the people currently in it meet its required levels on average — a percentage of required points achieved. Critical Fit is the same measure over critical skills only. Always read it next to Coverage.
Skill matrixA grid of people × skills with their current levels. Deactivated leavers are excluded from it, which is why its headcount can be one lower than the readiness report’s.
Org chartA graphical tree built from the data — manager line, supervisor line and the Site / Department / Service structure — with search and a Focus mode. Read-only: the hierarchy is edited on employee records, and the chart only reflects it.
Critical roleA role designated as essential. It is treated as critical from a criticality score of 4, and that is the unit of succession planning: below that score no succession plan is opened.
Succession bench & readiness bandThe named successors for a critical role, each with a band. The first three bands are computed from readiness (Ready-now ≥ 90 %, 1–2 yrs ≥ 70 %, otherwise 3+ yrs); “Emergency cover” is never assigned automatically — a human names it. An expired certificate drops the effective level to 0 in this calculation.
Risk-of-lossA per-person signal combining likelihood of leaving and impact of loss. Hard rules: nobody ever sees their own record, the 9-box key is stripped from the factors returned, and a manager is notified only when someone crosses into “high”, as a count, in-app.
Knowledge handoverA checklist of critical knowledge and transition tasks, auto-created from a leaver or mover event and auto-closed when every item is done.
Certification & VOCA recorded certificate or verification of competency, with a validity in months and a revalidation window (90 days by default). Alerts fire at 90 / 60 / 30 days and on the day it lapses, once per step. A requirement with nothing recorded reads NOT HELD — which means “nothing on file”, not “failed”. Employees see their own at My certifications, read-only: recording one is done by a manager or by compliance, with the evidence.
Coverage rule (safe-shift)An operational minimum: “this site or department must always have ≥ N people at level ≥ L in skill S, with a valid certificate”. Rules are evaluated hourly, alert only on a transition, and are projected 14 days ahead against planned absences and certificate expiries so a breach is announced before it happens.
LMS Hub & course–skill mappingThe integration console that connects an external learning system (Cornerstone, MyPath, or any xAPI/LTI LMS) and maps a course to the skill it builds, so a completion raises that skill. Configuring it requires the Configure LMS capability — it is an administrator surface, not a manager one. A reported completion is recorded as its own audited source and never overrides a supervisor review. on a sample instance no provider and no course were configured, so the completion loop was not exercised.
CalibrationA facilitated session where placements are reviewed side by side and adjusted with a mandatory rationale (the platform refuses an empty one). Finalising writes the result into both reading systems so they cannot diverge; a session with no adjustment at all is returned as “no adjustments” rather than stamped “finalised”.
Bias scanA sweep comparing each site and department against the mean. Below 5 placements overall or 3 per group it produces no alert and says the sample is too small — read that as “we do not know”, never as “nothing to report”.
Engagement survey / eNPSA pulse or engagement survey. Results are suppressed below a minimum response count so no individual is ever exposed, and they feed risk-of-loss.
DEI analyticsRepresentation, 9-box and PIP rates by group — always suppressed below a minimum group size; demographic data is sealed.
AI Copilot (on-prem)An assistant that answers questions about your people using ONLY data you are cleared to see, running on your own infrastructure behind an anonymisation firewall — no data leaves the estate. The About page states this as the deployment posture.
Local contentAn optional module, off by default: while featureLocalContent is disabled, /reports/local-content redirects to the dashboard for everyone, super admin included. A super admin enables it in Settings → General, together with one canonical spelling of the home country; a Nationality field then appears on employee records.
Manager digestA team summary — pending reviews, open IDP actions, measured critical gaps and, separately, critical skills not yet assessed, certificates due within 60 days, coverage breaches, campaign funnel. It runs on a configurable day and hour (digestDow, digestHour; Monday 07:00 by default) and sends nothing at all when there is nothing to say.
Report scheduleA saved report template + recipients + frequency, emailed automatically as a CSV attachment. It requires a template saved in the report builder first — with no template, no schedule can be created. Each run executes with the RBAC identity of the person who created it, which is exactly what determines what the recipients receive.
SnapshotA manual, named point-in-time copy you can restore in one click — take one before any risky change. Know its limits: a snapshot does not carry PIPs, IDPs, coaching, 9-box, disputes, lifecycle, notifications or the log, and restoring erases what it does not cover. Nightly automatic backups run separately (hour and retention set in Settings → Scheduled jobs; 0 = keep everything).
Append-only audit trailThe audit tables (assessment_history, system_logs, review_signatures) are append-only and SHA-256 hash-chained. The SQL console refuses to modify them, the telemetry pruner never touches the system log, and reverting a restore point does not rewind history: the chain is copied out and re-attached intact, with anything that can no longer attach quarantined rather than silently dropped.
Maker-checkerA second-pair-of-eyes control where one admin proposes a sensitive change and another approves it. In practice only one operation type is actually enrolled in the mechanism today (opening a PIP), and the approval queue was empty on a sample instance — do not plan controls around more than that.
Readiness thresholdThe percentage at which the role-ready verdict flips, 80 % by default, set in Settings → Readiness. Changing it moves every role-ready verdict in the platform at once: change it rarely, and announce it.

Frequently asked questions

A skill of mine shows “—” and “Not measured”. Is that a zero?
No — and the difference matters. “—” means nobody has assessed that skill yet: it is not counted as a gap, it does not lower your readiness, and it does not protect you either. It simply says nothing. A 0 is the opposite: somebody looked and recorded a nil level, and that is counted as a gap. On the same dashboard you can see both: “Training & Mentorship” at required 1 / current 0 / gap −1, and “Budget & Cost Control” at required 1 / current — / Not measured. Make a “—” disappear by rating the skill; that is the only way to get an honest figure.
Why does my self-assessment list 50 skills while my dashboard says 39/49?
Both numbers are right. Your role carries 50 requirements and every one of them is there to assess — the number of skills per role is designed by your department and nothing is removed from the list. But one of the 50 has a required level of 0, and a requirement of 0 can create neither a gap nor an achievement, so readiness is computed over the 49 that actually demand a level. The arithmetic then closes exactly: 39 met + 9 measured gaps + 1 not measured = 49, and 39/49 = 89 %.
My readiness says 100 % but my status says NOT READY. Which is wrong?
Neither — they answer two different questions on purpose. The readiness score is measured over what has actually been assessed: 100 % means everything that was looked at is at level. The role-ready verdict is measured over the whole job: every requirement, with the ones never assessed counted as 0, must reach the threshold (80 % by default) and every critical skill must be met. So “100 % — 2 / 13 — NOT READY” reads: excellent on the two skills we measured, and eleven we have never looked at. The fix is not a better rating, it is an assessment.
Can I attach a file or a certificate to my self-assessment?
No. The self-assessment form has no file upload: the columns are Skill, Required level, Your self-rating, Status and Notes. Use the Notes box to give a concrete example in writing — that is what a reviewer reads, and it is what makes a rating approved quickly and disputed rarely. Certificates and VOCs are recorded separately, and not by you: your manager or compliance records them with the supporting evidence, and you see the result on My certifications.
What is the difference between a supervisor and a manager?
They are TWO distinct roles, carried by two different fields on the employee record: “supervisor” on one side, “manager” on the other. The supervisor instructs the file: opens the review, comments, sends it back, enters their own rating, approves or rejects, drafts the 9-Box placement and runs coaching. The manager does all of that, and only they can additionally: validate a file already reviewed by the supervisor, arbitrate a disagreement, and approve, reject, archive or disclose a 9-Box placement. There is no reverse — no supervisor action is closed to a manager.

Mind one interface trap: both profiles show the same “Manager” badge and exactly the same menu. The difference is invisible on screen and shows up only when acting, as a 403 “Not authorized: manager/admin only” for the supervisor. An administrator who fills in a “supervisor” thinking they are granting manager powers grants neither manager validation, nor arbitration, nor 9-Box approval.

Also worth knowing: a self-assessment goes first to the supervisor; with none set it goes to the manager; with neither it falls into the relevant administrator’s queue. And the manager’s span contains their supervisors’ — on the demonstration data, 16 people for the manager against 15 for the supervisor, the supervisor herself included.
Why does the Approve button work for me but the API says “manager/admin only”?
Because they are not the same action. The review console offers three verbs — Approve, Request changes, Reject — and all three are open to anyone who governs the person, supervisor included; that is how a supervisor finalises a reviewed file in the screen today. The two manager-only verbs, manager validation and arbitration, are API endpoints with no button in the console at present: they are verified to work, but you will not find them on the page. The one manager exclusivity you can actually click is on the 9-Box: approve, reject, archive and disclose.
My password keeps being refused
The rule is at least 12 characters, with an uppercase, a lowercase, a number and a special character — announced under the field and enforced by the server, so a shorter one is refused however you submit it. Length alone is not enough either: common words, keyboard patterns and sequences (“abcd”, “1234”, “qwerty”) are rejected even beyond 12 characters. A long, unique passphrase satisfies the rule with no memory effort. Note that changing your password signs out every other session.
I forgot my password / I am locked out
Open /forgot-password (the “Forgot your password?” link is shown on the sign-in page when email sending is configured; the page itself is reachable either way). Enter your username or email and a reset link is sent — to the address recorded on your record, which is the one you set yourself in My profile. The answer is deliberately the same whether the identifier exists or not, so no account is revealed. If a banner says email sending is not configured, or you never filled in your email, an administrator has to reset it for you — which is exactly why you should fill in your email before you need it.
Where do I set my email address?
Account menu → My profile (/account), a page every employee has since 3.22.85. Email and phone are the only two fields you can change there; your name, employee number, username, role, site, department, service and supervisor are managed by your administrator and are refused if submitted — report an error in those to your manager or HR administrator.
Can I see the devices signed in to my account?
Yes — every employee can, not just administrators. Account menu → Active Sessions (/account/sessions) lists each device with its IP, sign-in time and last activity, marks the one you are using as THIS DEVICE, and offers “Sign out all other sessions (N)”. Make it a reflex after using a shared machine. If you see a session you do not recognise, sign the others out and change your password.
Can I turn off notification emails without losing anything?
Yes. Account menu → Notifications (/account/notifications): switching email off keeps everything in the in-app bell, it only stops the email copy. The same page has quiet hours — set both times or leave both empty — during which nothing appears and nothing is sent; nothing is dropped, it is all delivered when the window ends.
Can I change my self-assessment after submitting?
Yes — update your ratings and submit again; it simply goes back to your reviewer, with no duplicate submission. If a reviewer asks for changes, the line comes back to you with their comment. Once approved, the reviewer’s rating — not your self-rating — becomes your official current level.
My reviewer gave me a different rating from mine. Can they just do that?
They can, and they must justify it: the review console pre-fills their rating with yours, and the moment they change it a dialog demands a short written reason — the save is refused without one, and the reason is kept and visible in the review. If you still disagree, open a dispute from your reviews page: it climbs a timed ladder (supervisor → manager → HR arbitration) so it cannot stall, and the final decided level is shown to you.
My coaching plan will not save
A coaching or mentoring plan must be anchored to a context — an IDP, a PIP, or a specific skill gap — and the platform refuses a plan without one; an inline message points to the field. That rule exists so coaching always targets a named need rather than a vague intention.
The 9-box created a PIP / an IDP by itself
Yes — approval is the trigger point. Low performance, whatever the potential (three cells, not just the low/low corner), opens a 90-day PIP with a paired coaching plan. High potential with medium performance, or medium potential with high performance, opens a 180-day IDP. The top-right “Gold Star” corner triggers nothing. Nothing is duplicated: if a plan is already open, the existing one is adopted.
Why can I see a menu entry but get “Access denied” on it?
There are two independent locks and you need both: the capability says what you may do, the scope says on whom. The refusal page names which one stopped you — “Missing capability”, with the capability code, its description and who can grant it, or “Outside your scope”, which says in as many words that nothing is missing from your capabilities: it is the perimeter. A third form exists for the six strictly super-admin screens (access review, API keys, license, sessions, SSO, SQL console): they redirect to the dashboard instead of showing a refusal. Your own page My access (/mon-acces) lists all 31 capabilities with what you hold.
I gave a local admin a perimeter but they still cannot do anything
That is expected, and it is by design: a scope without a capability does nothing, and a capability without a scope acts on nobody. A brand-new delegate starts with no capability at all until you tick some. The Admins page even counts the cases for you in a banner (“N account(s) have a perimeter but no capability — they cannot do anything”). Grant capabilities on their record, remembering that each configuration area is split read/write, and set a duration — the form defaults to 12 months rather than permanent.
If I grant someone admin access, do they still sign in with SSO?
No, and this is worth reading before you click. Granting admin access creates a separate admin account. Administrators sign in with a password plus two-factor authentication, not by SSO — so you must set their admin password on the Admins page afterwards, or they cannot get in. Their SSO identity stays on their employee account, which they keep for their business view. One person, two accounts, on purpose.
An import said “unchanged” everywhere. Did it do nothing?
It did exactly what it should: it compared and found nothing to write. The counters are deliberately explicit — created (inserted), updated (existed with other values, rewritten), unchanged (already matched, nothing written), skipped (not applied, reason listed in the errors) and not rated (cells left blank in the template). “Unchanged” is the honest answer to a re-import, and “not rated” is never turned into a level 0. Always run Preview first: it validates every row and writes nothing.
Is my data backed up?
Yes, on two layers. An automatic nightly database backup runs at the hour set in Settings → Scheduled jobs and keeps the last N files (0 means keep everything); its date and status are published on the About page. Separately, an administrator can take a named snapshot before a risky change and restore it in one click — but a snapshot does not carry PIPs, IDPs, coaching, 9-box, disputes, lifecycle, notifications or the audit log, and restoring erases what it does not cover. A backup you have never restored is a hope, not a plan.
Where is the Local Content report? I cannot see it.
It is an optional module and it is off by default: while it is off, /reports/local-content redirects to the dashboard for everyone, super admin included — a missing screen here is a switch, not a missing feature. A super admin enables it in Settings → General (featureLocalContent) and fills the home country there too, using one canonical spelling. A Nationality field then appears on employee records.
Is there an HR role? Who settles a dispute nobody answered?
There is no separate “HR” account type — the platform is manager-owned by design. HR is modelled as a local admin holding the Arbitrate disputes capability, and that person is the final (L2) arbiter for disputes a manager did not resolve in time. If no arbiter acts within the L2 SLA, the dispute auto-finalises on the supervisor’s rating, the reason is annotated “[auto-finalized: no HR decision within SLA]”, the employee is told their contested rating is now official, and the whole thing is logged. Everything stays inside the system and audited; nothing is handled off-platform.
As a manager, what can I do on a campaign?
Since September 2026 you open the campaign console (/cycles) and see your team there: who has not started, who is in progress, who is waiting on your review, and how many days the campaign is overdue. You can chase — one report at a time, or "Chase all my non-starters" — and the same person is only chased once a day. Your landing page carries the item "N reports have not started campaign X". What stays with administration is the campaign itself: launching, extending, reopening, locking and closing decide what an entire population is measured on, and excusing someone (a written reason, kept in the log) belongs to an administrator holding "Manage campaigns" for their scope.
I am a manager but I get no weekly digest
Three things decide it. The digest runs on a configurable day and hour (Monday 07:00 by default) — check those before assuming it is broken. It sends nothing at all when there is nothing to say, so an empty week produces no message, which is the intended behaviour rather than a failure. And for the email copy you need SMTP configured, the digest email category enabled, and an email address on your record; without those you still get the in-app copy in the 🔔 bell.
I built a report but cannot schedule it
A schedule sends a saved template, so save your report as a template in the report builder first — with no template the Schedules page says exactly that and offers nothing to pick. Then choose recipients (up to 20 valid addresses) and a frequency. The attachment format is CSV. Each run executes with the RBAC identity of whoever created the schedule, so recipients receive exactly what that person is allowed to see — check that before adding an audience.
A dashboard shows zeros everywhere. Is the organisation empty?
Check your scope before your conclusions. An account with no perimeter sees a real population of zero: 0 employees covered, an empty skill matrix, “No sites found”, and a health score computed on nobody. Those zeros describe the perimeter, not the organisation. Reference data still shows — the framework, its pillars, sub-domains and roles are visible because they are not people data. If someone is meant to read numbers, give them a scope; without one the dashboard is not wrong, it is empty.
How do I remove a review, cancel a self-assessment, or cancel a plan that was raised in error?
As a SuperAdmin, from Administration → Maintenance (/admin/maintenance). Nothing is ever deleted there — each action is a state plus a written reason. On a self-assessment the button depends on its state: Withdraw the review while it is under review, sent back or reviewed by the supervisor (the review is withdrawn, the file returns to submitted exactly as the employee filed it and re-enters the reviewer’s queue); Request a change once it is approved (the approval and the lock are lifted, it goes back to the employee); Cancel for one that should not exist at all (it moves to rejected). The official skill level that was promoted is never rewritten by any of the three — it stays until a new approval replaces it. IDPs, PIPs and 9-box positions are cancelled from the same page, and an employee record entered twice is voided (reversible — not a departure, not an erasure). Every action bypasses the two-person cancellation rule on purpose and says so in the System Logs, on the movement feed and in the record’s own trail. Two things the panel refuses: a review under dispute (resolve the dispute first) and an assessment in arbitration (the manager decides it).
Can two accounts share the same e-mail address?
Yes. An e-mail address is contact information, not an identity: a person may hold several accounts (an employee record and an admin account, two records across entities, a shared departmental mailbox). When you type an address that is already in use — on an employee, an admin, your own profile, or the Invitations console — the screen advises you (“already used by …”) and lets you continue; nothing is refused. The advisory names only the accounts you are allowed to see and counts the rest. Two things follow from it: sign in with your username when your address is shared (signing in by e-mail is refused when it would have to guess between accounts, and the audit line says why), and the forgot-password mail sends one link per account, each naming the account it resets. Single sign-on never auto-links a shared address either — the intended account is linked by its external id.
How do I switch language or theme?
Use the FR/EN switcher in the app (sidebar / top bar); your choice is remembered on that browser. Use the 🌙/☀️ icon for the dark or light theme. This guide has its own English / Français selector at the top — if it opens in the other language, switch it there.
